OLD RED DWARFS

The universe is not yet old enough to contain an old red dwarf. A star of a tenth of a solar mass burns its hydrogen for trillions of years — hundreds of times the present age of the universe — and what becomes of it was worked out by calculation in “The End of the Main Sequence” (Laughlin, Bodenheimer & Adams 1997): the smallest stars never become red giants; they grow steadily hotter, briefly outshine their entire prior lives, and die as helium white dwarfs. This page carries a working recreation of that calculation — a FORTRAN 77 descendant of the same 1964 Henyey code that runs on the companion page, rebuilt from the published physics: the Saumon–Chabrier–Van Horn equation of state, the molecule-and-grain opacities, the full pp-chain and CNO reaction network with ³He followed out of equilibrium, and a grey radiative atmosphere. A star is born high on the Hayashi track and integrated until the fuel is gone. The trillion years take a few seconds. Run one now.

3–1The run

No punched cards this time — by 1997 the input was two lines in a file. The first line carries the star (mass, hydrogen, metals, initial ³He); the second the run (mesh, models, time step, tolerances, mixing length, and three switches for the equation of state, the opacity, and the starting point). The presets are the paper's own cast of characters.

3–4The physics

  
The solver is the Henyey method of 1964, unchanged in spirit: four
     difference equations on a Lagrangian mesh, linearized and solved
     simultaneously by Newton–Raphson with a block elimination. Around that
     skeleton this line carries the physics of the 1997 paper. The equation of
     state is the Saumon–Chabrier–Van Horn (1995) tables for hydrogen and
     helium — the original distributed tables, interpolated with their own
     published derivatives so the surface is smooth in exactly the way Newton
     demands — mixed by additive volumes, with the 1965 Berkeley degeneracy
     formula taking over above the tables' ceiling. The low-temperature opacity
     is the molecule-and-grain Rosseland grid of Alexander, Johnson & Rypma
     (1983), whose water-vapor blanketing and grain condensation set the very
     shape of a red dwarf. The interior radiative opacity is the King IVa
     table of Cox & Tabor (1976) — the source the 1993 paper names —
     and the conduction that governs the degenerate endgame comes from the
     Hubbard & Lampe (1969) tables, both transcribed digit by digit
     from the journal scans. The nuclear network follows the 1965 Appendix C
     scheme with all seven species explicit — H, ³He, ⁴He, C, ¹³C, N, O — so the
     ³He bump and the CN-cycle equilibration happen on their own schedules
     rather than by assumption. The atmosphere is the grey radiative model of
     Laughlin & Bodenheimer (1993), case B: hydrostatic equilibrium
     iterated with the real equation of state down the optical-depth scale,
     mixing-length convection through the superadiabatic layers, adiabatic
     below.

  
Two numerical habits of this line deserve their footnote. The
     backward-Euler composition step uses the conjugate quadratic root
     2(−C)/(B + √(B²−4AC)) — the schoolbook root is a catastrophic
     cancellation at cool mesh points, and its rounding noise once destroyed
     hydrogen ten times faster than the star could shine. And the first static
     model is attempted from a soft start and retried once with the pseudoflux
     normalized to the luminosity guess: stars with radiative interiors need
     the boost, fully convective dwarfs refuse it, and the machine now simply
     tries both. The shell-burning endgame added three more habits: the
     “temporary points” of the 1964 paper, splitting mesh zones where the
     shell source steepens; a damping rule that collapses the Newton
     two-cycle a sharp composition front sets up; and convective-boundary
     hysteresis that persists across time steps, so a marginal zone cannot
     flip the pattern every model and masquerade as a hard problem.
     Calibrated against the Sun, the same physics gives
     L = 1.000 L☉, R = 1.001 R☉,
     Teff = 5782 K at the solar age — at the
     hydrogen abundance X = 0.735 that pre-OPAL opacities
     historically demanded.

The offsets are systematic and understood — and, remarkably, they are
     not in the interior. Installing the paper's own King IVa interior
     opacity moved these dwarfs by nothing at all, to five digits: a fully
     convective star's structure depends only on its photosphere and its
     adiabat, so the brightness offset lives in the atmospheric treatment,
     where the 1997 models (with their fuller molecular data) ran cooler
     than this grey-plus-AJR stack. LBA97 sat cool and dim of the observed
     M dwarfs, as the paper candidly noted; these models sit near the
     modern temperature scale, with the 1997 track between. The structure of
     the story survives everything: the two-gigayear contraction lands on
     the paper's own number; the ³He peak and core expansion arrive on a
     clock uniformly scaled by the brightness ratio; the hydrogen-burning
     boundary falls between 0.08 and 0.10 M☉ — press RUN on the 0.08
     preset and watch a star fail to become one.

  
And the endgame no longer guards the frontier. With the
     Hubbard–Lampe conduction installed and the shell-transition numerics
     tamed, the complete-life preset runs the whole biography in one job:
     hydrogen exhaustion at 3.2 trillion years, the degenerate core held at
     thirty thousand grams per cubic centimeter, the blueward excursion
     cresting at 5906 K against the paper's published maximum of
     5807 K, the corner, and the cooling descent to a millionth of the
     solar luminosity at 1250 K — past the published endpoint of the
     1997 paper's Figure 1. The cooling clock below ~1700 K is
     provisional — that regime rests on table extensions where the
     still-paywalled grain opacities would rule — which is the honest edge
     of what this recreation currently knows.

  
The engine is the repository's f77/henyey77.f,
     translated to C by a freshly built f2c and carried into your browser by
     WebAssembly with the equation-of-state tables embedded — the trillion-year
     integration runs entirely on your side of the wire. The full head-to-head,
     with radii and central densities, is in the repository's
     plan file.
